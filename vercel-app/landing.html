Why this exists

San Francisco's restaurant memory is evaporating.

Kitchens close every month, and when one closes, decades of culinary lineage vanish with it: who trained there, where they went, what they carried with them. The Chronicle tracks the closures; nobody tracked the lineage.

On the other side of the table, a resident spending real money on dinner can look up vibe and service in seconds — but a kitchen's craft pedigree was unlook-upable. Bone Fides fixes both halves with one artifact: a cited, growing map of who cooked where.

How it works

Reads the journalism. Cites every move.

01

Public food journalism, nothing else

2,221 articles from Eater SF, SFGATE, the Chronicle and independent writers — closures, openings, chef-shuffle roundups, profiles. No review data, no proprietary anything.

02

Reading, with structure

Each article is read the way a food editor would, extracting structured moves — person, from-kitchen, to-kitchen, role — plus the evidence sentence that justifies them.

03

Search that survives nicknames

Search resolves your query to the right kitchen — "Tartine" and "Tartine Bakery" land in the same place, and homonymous chefs get flagged.

04

A dossier with receipts

Every move carries a confidence dot and a link to its source. The lineage score weighs how well documented the kitchen is — outlet authority, corroboration, depth. If a claim is thin, the dossier says so.

Who it's for

For the people who live here.

The resident booking Friday dinner can finally check a kitchen's papers, not just its stars. The loyal regular watching a favorite close can follow the chefs to what's next. The curious can see how the city's kitchens are one long conversation — Tartine alumni scattered across the Bay, the Betelnut thread running through China Live and Asia Live, a generation of cooks tracing back to the same few stoves.

And the industry economics are real: in an industry bleeding out, visibility of genuine lineage is survival. The chef who came up under a master finally has a legible advantage over a marketing budget.

The next time a kitchen closes, ask its bone fides.
